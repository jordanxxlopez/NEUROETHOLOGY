## 2
**MDMA**, 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine, altered social approach in the California two-spot octopus, _Octopus bimaculoides_. Edsinger and Dölen compared exploration before and after drug exposure. A three-chamber aquarium offered access to an unfamiliar male, an unfamiliar object and an empty central compartment. The other octopus remained inside a perforated container. This arrangement permitted social investigation while preventing a fight between the animals.

After MDMA treatment, subjects spent more time in the compartment containing the male. Their time near the unfamiliar object did not increase correspondingly. Transitions between compartments also failed to increase. The behavioral shift therefore concerned the social stimulus rather than a general rise in swimming activity. The same animal supplied a pre-treatment comparison for its later exploration.

The experimental change included direct investigation of the container holding the other octopus. Before treatment, contact was often restricted to an extended arm. After treatment, animals used more extensive contact involving the ventral body surface. **Social approach** here means measurable proximity and contact with another individual. The experiment connects a psychoactive substance to a change in an otherwise predominantly solitary animal’s social behavior.
## 3
The identity of a social partner affected untreated octopus exploration. Both male and female subjects approached an unfamiliar female more readily than an unfamiliar male. A **conspecific** is another individual of the same species. The study compared conspecific investigation with exploration of a novel object. Partner sex consequently formed part of the biological stimulus rather than an interchangeable feature of the apparatus.

The perforated container allowed visual, tactile and chemical information to pass between animals. The subject could approach or withdraw throughout the 30 min session. Investigators measured time in each compartment separately. Time in the central compartment was similar across the partner-sex conditions. Differences were concentrated in the relative allocation of exploration to social and object compartments.

MDMA testing used the less-preferred male social stimulus. Drug exposure increased the time allocated to that compartment and changed the pattern of bodily contact. Proximity and touch therefore supplied complementary descriptions of the response. The object compartment retained a separate measure of non-social exploration. Social behavior in this preparation depended jointly on the partner being encountered and the subject’s pharmacological state.
## 4
**Serotonin** is a signaling molecule used by neurons in many animal lineages. A **transporter** is a membrane protein that moves a substance across the cell membrane. The serotonin transporter, abbreviated **SERT**, participates in clearing released serotonin from extracellular space. MDMA interacts with this transporter in mammals. The octopus study examined whether the corresponding protein retained relevant molecular features.

The investigators identified two octopus copies of the transporter gene family corresponding to vertebrate SLC6A4. Their analysis compared protein sequences across animal groups. The transporter contains multiple membrane-spanning segments that form a binding pocket. A short region of transmembrane segment 6 implicated in MDMA binding was identical in the compared human and octopus sequences. Other parts of the proteins were more divergent.

The molecular comparison and the behavioral experiment concerned different levels of organization. Sequence conservation identified a plausible shared drug target. Pharmacological treatment then altered social investigation in the living octopus. The study proposes that ancient serotonergic machinery can modulate social behavior despite major differences in brain organization. The precise octopus neurons mediating the response were not localized by these experiments.
## 5
Honeybees, _Apis mellifera_, encounter **caffeine**, a plant alkaloid, in some floral nectars. An **alkaloid** is a nitrogen-containing natural compound that can affect animal physiology. Wright and colleagues paired a floral odor with a sucrose reward. Some rewards also contained caffeine within the range found in nectar. The learned response was extension of the proboscis, the bee’s feeding apparatus.

During testing, the odor was presented without the reward. A bee that extended its proboscis had retained the odor–reward association. Caffeine-treated bees were more likely to respond at later memory tests than bees trained with sucrose alone. The difference persisted at 24 h and 72 h. At the longer retention intervals, caffeine approximately tripled the likelihood of remembering the rewarded scent.

The treatment changed memory even though the odor itself was unchanged. The plant-derived compound accompanied ingestion of the reward during learning. Later odor presentation could then guide behavior after caffeine was no longer being supplied as a reward. **Retention** refers to the persistence of a learned association over time. A nectar constituent can therefore alter the future attractiveness of a flower’s sensory identity.

## 7

Acceptance declined as the concentration rose above the natural nectar range.

The feeding assay measured proboscis extension after contact with the solution. Increasing sucrose concentration changed the acceptance of caffeine-containing solutions. Sweetness and the deterrent compound therefore contributed jointly to the feeding response. Caffeine above approximately 1 mM was increasingly rejected in the tested mixtures. The same chemical could enhance memory at lower concentrations and discourage consumption at higher concentrations.

These effects act at different stages of a foraging visit. Acceptance determines whether a bee consumes the nectar that it encounters. The measured nectar concentrations occupy a range in which pharmacological effects can occur without strong feeding deterrence.
## 8

Bees previously given repeated low-dose cocaine were less affected by the later challenge. Their locomotor impairment was reduced relative to drug-naive comparison bees. Vehicle-treated and sham-handled groups separated the drug effect from application and handling. The repeated exposure was administered by the investigators. This experiment measured physiological adaptation rather than voluntary selection of cocaine.

Cocaine interferes with the reuptake of **biogenic amines**, transmitters including dopamine, serotonin and octopamine in insects. Behavioral adaptation can occur while the total amount measured across the brain remains similar. Local transmitter release and receptor responses are distinct from the size of the whole-brain chemical pool.
## 9
Bumblebees, _Bombus terrestris_, encountered nicotine in an artificial-flower choice task. **Nicotine** is a plant alkaloid that acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. Baracchi and colleagues offered flower types with equal sucrose rewards. One type also contained nicotine and the other did not. This arrangement separated the compound’s contribution from a difference in the available sugar reward.

The investigators tested 1 ppm, 2.5 ppm and 50 ppm nicotine. Here **ppm**, parts per million, expresses the concentration of nicotine in the solution. Bees preferred the 1 ppm nicotine-associated flowers over equally rewarded nicotine-free alternatives. At 2.5 ppm, their choices were closer to an equal division. The much higher 50 ppm concentration deterred visits to the nicotine-containing flowers.

Low and high concentrations consequently produced different patterns of choice. Nectar-relevant nicotine concentrations were not automatically rejected as toxins. High concentrations reduced attraction even though sugar remained available. The preference experiment measured repeated visits by freely moving individual bees. A flower’s pharmacological composition can alter foraging choices independently of its caloric reward.

Drug exposure during feeding therefore changed the development of a sensory association used during foraging.

Prior nicotine exposure increased the response to a later nicotine challenge in zebrafish larvae. The investigators called this effect **sensitization**, an enhanced response following earlier treatment. Acute responsiveness and sensitization appeared at different developmental stages. Five- and six-day-old larvae could express the sensitized response. Four-day-old larvae responded acutely but did not yet exhibit the same sensitization.

The study tested whether glutamate receptor signaling contributed to this adaptation. **Glutamate** is an excitatory transmitter, and NMDA receptors are one class of its receptors. The antagonist APV interferes with NMDA-receptor activation. Coadministration during nicotine exposure prevented the development of sensitization. The intervention separated the response to nicotine from the processes that changed later responsiveness.

Repeated exposure consequently recruited more than the receptor directly activated by nicotine. Cholinergic signaling supplied the initial drug-sensitive pathway. NMDA-receptor involvement connected prior exposure with a later behavioral change. The locomotor recordings retain the assay’s swimming movements and temporal pattern. Behavioral history and developmental stage determined how strongly the same nicotine concentration altered movement.
## 36
Petzold and colleagues used the nicotine assay to identify genes affecting drug responsiveness. A **forward genetic screen** begins with altered behavior and then identifies the associated genetic change. Gene-breaking transposons disrupted selected genes while supplying fluorescent tags. A **transposon** is a DNA element that can insert into another genomic location. The tag allowed researchers to track inherited insertions through families.

The screen excluded lines with obvious baseline swimming defects. Remaining candidates were tested for altered acute and sensitized nicotine responses. One affected gene was **cct8**, which encodes a component of a protein-folding complex. Its mutant line was named bette davis. These fish had reduced nicotine responses while retaining responses to several other tested stimuli.

The investigators followed the insertion through breeding and molecular analysis. They measured the reduction in intact gene transcript and examined its expression in neural tissue. These steps connected the behavioral phenotype to a defined genomic location. The source figures preserve neural expression and the resulting movement-response comparisons. A drug-responsive behavior can identify cellular machinery beyond the receptor to which the drug initially binds.
## 37
A second zebrafish nicotine-response mutation affected **gabbr1.2**, a gene encoding a GABA-B receptor subunit. GABA-B receptors signal through G proteins rather than forming the rapidly opening channel characteristic of GABA-A receptors. A **G protein** relays receptor activation to intracellular signaling pathways. The mutant line was named humphrey bogart. Its nicotine response was strongly reduced despite relatively preserved baseline movement.

The gene-breaking insertion also supplied a red fluorescent reporter. Neural labeling located expression associated with the disrupted locus. Researchers then used **Cre recombinase**, an enzyme that cuts DNA at specified recognition sites, to remove the disruptive cassette. Reversing the insertion restored the nicotine-response phenotype toward normal. This reversal supplied a direct test linking the genomic intervention with behavior.

The original three-dimensional recording contains the red reporter-labeled neural preparation. Its fluorescence identifies the cells carrying the tagged expression pattern. The behavioral rescue and anatomical expression address complementary questions about the affected pathway. GABA-B signaling contributed to how the larva responded to a cholinergic drug. Nicotine responsiveness therefore depended on interactions among transmitter systems rather than on nicotinic receptors alone.
## 38
Prairie voles, _Microtus ochrogaster_, can maintain a preference for a familiar mating partner. Walcott and Ryabinin allowed pairs to cohabit for 1 week before manipulating alcohol access. Males then received access to water and 10% ethanol. Their partners either received the same choice or water alone. A control condition supplied water to both members of the pair.

**Concordant drinking** means that both partners have access to alcohol in this design. **Discordant drinking** means that alcohol access differs between partners. The comparison changed the social context of the male’s drinking. Males whose partners also received alcohol consumed more than males whose partners received only water. Amount consumed and the later social preference were measured separately.

The partner-preference test offered a familiar partner and an unfamiliar female. Investigators measured time spent huddling with each animal. Huddling is sustained close bodily contact rather than simple occupancy of the same chamber. All treatment groups retained a measurable partner preference. The strength of that preference was lower when only the male had alcohol access.
## 39
Discordant drinking reduced male prairie voles’ partner preference relative to the comparison conditions. The effect occurred after the relationship had already formed. The experiment therefore examined **pair-bond maintenance**, the persistence of an established selective attachment. It differed from testing whether a new pair bond could form during intoxication. The partner’s drinking condition altered the outcome of the male’s alcohol exposure.

Both-drinking pairs retained a stronger partner preference than pairs in which only the male drank. Alcohol consumption alone consequently did not predict the social outcome. Researchers also measured drinking outside the immediate presence of the partner. The preference difference remained associated with the treatment history. The social effect reflected more than the male merely approaching another animal during simultaneous drinking.

The study examined **oxytocin**, a neuropeptide involved in social regulation, in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus. Alcohol-exposed males had reduced oxytocin immunoreactivity in this region. This reduction occurred regardless of the female partner’s drinking status. **Immunoreactivity** measures the signal detected by an antibody directed at the target molecule. The shared oxytocin change therefore differed from the partner-dependent behavioral pattern.
## 40
Aggression and partner preference were assessed with different social tests in the prairie vole study. A **resident–intruder test** places an unfamiliar animal into the subject’s environment. Researchers counted aggressive responses toward a stranger male. Aggression frequency did not differ clearly across the drinking conditions. The partner-preference difference therefore did not generalize to every measured social behavior.

The researchers also examined FosB-family protein signals in the **periaqueductal gray**, a midbrain region surrounding the cerebral aqueduct. This molecular pattern corresponded more closely to the partner-dependent preference effect than did the shared oxytocin reduction. The behavioral comparisons nevertheless separated selective contact with a partner from aggression toward a stranger. ## 41
Socially housed cynomolgus macaques, _Macaca fascicularis_, learned to choose between drug-associated and food-associated options. Roberts and colleagues examined **reinstatement**, the return of a previously reduced drug-associated response. Saline replaced drug during extinction sessions. **Extinction** is the decline in responding when its previous consequence is removed. Later drug pretreatment tested whether the response returned.

Cocaine and **cocaethylene** both increased choice of the previously drug-associated option. Cocaethylene is an active metabolite formed when cocaine and ethanol are used together. Cocaine produced reinstatement at lower doses than cocaethylene in the comparison. The study examined socially dominant and subordinate animals of both sexes. Dominant animals generally displayed greater reinstatement than subordinates under these conditions.

Social rank was therefore relevant to a specific stage of drug-related behavior. The measured outcome was renewed drug-associated choice after extinction. It was not a general ranking of which social group was always most vulnerable to every drug effect. Food remained an available alternative during the choice procedure. The assay connected social position with competition between a drug-associated action and another reinforced action.
## 42
Nicotine altered cocaine-related reinstatement differently in male and female macaques. Roberts and colleagues tested nicotine alone and together with cocaine or cocaethylene. The drug-associated choice measure was the same response used during extinction and reinstatement. Nicotine alone increased that choice primarily in females. Its effect consequently depended on the subject’s sex and the existing history of the drug-associated option.

Adding nicotine increased cocaine-induced drug-associated choice in females more consistently than in males. The tested nicotine range was 0.01–0.056 mg/kg. The corresponding interaction with cocaethylene did not produce the same clear group effect. **Polysubstance exposure** means exposure to more than one psychoactive substance. Interactions between substances were specific rather than interchangeable across the tested combinations.

Social rank did not account for the female-selective nicotine effect. Both dominant and subordinate females could display enhanced cocaine-related responding. Sex and rank therefore contributed differently to the experimental outcomes. The task retained food as the competing alternative rather than measuring movement alone. A social and biological context can alter which action is selected after renewed exposure to a drug combination.
## 43
A single ethanol intoxication altered neuronal proteins and organization in mice. Knabbe and colleagues first measured proteins associated with hippocampal synapses. The molecular screen therefore led to measurements of a defined neuronal structure.

The study also followed organelles within cortical axons using repeated microscopy. **Mitochondria** support cellular energy metabolism and can move along axons. Baseline imaging established which organelles were stationary or mobile before treatment. The source recording preserves this transport before the ethanol-associated change. Measurements within the same neuronal stretches connected the acute exposure with subsequent cellular dynamics.
## 44
Ethanol increased the fraction of axonal mitochondria that moved in the mouse imaging experiment. Investigators repeatedly imaged labeled axons in the motor cortex. The **motor cortex** is a cerebral cortical region involved in movement control. Saline and ethanol sessions provided within-animal comparisons. Images were collected repeatedly over the hours following treatment.

The increase in mitochondrial mobility became apparent after approximately 60 min. It reached roughly twice the baseline level around 180 min. The effect persisted for at least 4 h but was absent at the 24 h measurement. **Mobility** here refers to the proportion of mitochondria moving during observation. The velocity of moving mitochondria did not increase in parallel.

The distinction separates recruitment into movement from faster transport once movement begins. A stationary mitochondrion can become mobile without changing the speed of the moving population. ## 45

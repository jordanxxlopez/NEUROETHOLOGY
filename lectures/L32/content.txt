Song is a learned, organized motor sequence|nottebohm76|n13
**Song** consists of coordinated vocal elements whose frequency and timing can be measured with a spectrogram, a display of acoustic energy across time and frequency. Nottebohm and colleagues recorded adult male canaries before brain surgery, establishing each bird's own repertoire as the behavioral baseline.
They compared postoperative vocalizations with those recordings rather than treating all changes in singing as equivalent. Damage could reduce the number of available syllables, disturb their acoustic structure, or disrupt the organization of phrases while leaving vocal output possible.
A bird that still vocalizes can therefore have a severe song-control deficit. These experiments localized components of an established motor behavior; adult lesions alone did not identify where a juvenile stores or learns a tutor's song.

Song disruption depends on lesion location|nottebohm76|n11
A **lesion** is damage to tissue used to test whether a region contributes to a behavior. Nottebohm and colleagues made unilateral electrolytic lesions in adult male canaries and reconstructed their positions histologically after repeated postoperative song recordings.
Lesions involving HVC or the robust nucleus of the arcopallium, **RA**, severely altered song organization, whereas several lesions outside the implicated pathway had little or no effect. Comparing effective and ineffective lesion sites constrained the anatomical interpretation beyond a simple surgery-versus-no-surgery comparison.
Electrolytic current can damage both local neurons and passing axons. The results establish that these restricted forebrain territories are necessary for normal adult song, but do not isolate a particular cell type or prove that every nucleus performs the same computation.

Degenerating axons reveal descending song pathways|nottebohm76|n7
An **axon** carries signals away from a neuron; a projection is an axonal connection between regions. A **synapse** is a junction through which a neuron influences another cell. Nottebohm and colleagues combined behavioral lesions with Fink–Heimer staining, a method that reveals degenerating fibers and terminals downstream of damaged neurons.

The historical names in the original article differ from current avian terminology: HVC is now used as a proper name, and RA lies in the arcopallium. Degeneration tracing establishes a pathway's anatomical direction, but does not measure its transmitter, synaptic sign, or activity during singing.

A **projection neuron** sends an axon outside its local region. Benezra and colleagues distinguished HVC neurons projecting to RA, designated HVC(RA), from neurons projecting to Area X, a song-related **basal ganglia** region involved in action control, designated HVC(X).

**Two-photon microscopy** uses nonlinear excitation to resolve fluorescent structures within tissue. A **dendrite** is a neuronal process that receives synaptic input. Benezra and colleagues compared reconstructed dendritic trees of HVC(RA) and HVC(X) neurons after registering their positions within HVC, allowing cell types to be compared in a common anatomical frame.

Benezra and colleagues quantified dendritic length along three spatial axes and the total path length of reconstructed HVC neurons. Centering these measurements on each cell body separated dendritic extent from the neuron's absolute position within the nucleus.

A **broadcast arbor** distributes axonal branches across a relatively broad volume, whereas a local arbor remains more spatially restricted. Benezra and colleagues quantified HVC axonal extent and the proportion of the nucleus occupied by each reconstructed neuron.

RA neurons generate exceptionally narrow spikes|sodium21|z1
An **action potential** is a rapid electrical spike produced by voltage-dependent membrane conductances. An **ion channel** is a membrane protein that permits ions to cross the membrane. Zemel and colleagues recorded identified RA projection neurons in zebra finch brain slices using whole-cell current clamp, which measures membrane voltage while current is controlled.
At approximately 40 °C, near avian physiological temperature, spontaneous spikes had a half-width of about 0.18 ms. Spontaneous firing was approximately fourfold faster than at room temperature, and depolarizing current could evoke sustained rapid spike trains.
Temperature therefore changes the quantitative description of these motor neurons. Slice recordings establish intrinsic firing capacity under controlled conditions, but are not recordings of the commands issued during a particular song syllable; intact singing additionally supplies patterned synaptic input and behavioral state.

Resurgent sodium current follows repolarization|sodium21|z2
A **resurgent sodium current** is an inward sodium current that appears as a membrane repolarizes after strong depolarization. Zemel and colleagues used voltage clamp, which controls membrane voltage while recording current, to separate this component from the initial transient sodium current.
A step from −90 to +30 mV activated transient current; subsequent test voltages elicited resurgent current, with a peak near −3 nA at −45 mV in adult RA projection neurons. The sequence of voltage steps made the later inward current experimentally distinguishable from the initial spike-generating component.
Resurgent current can support renewed excitation during recovery from a spike. The recording establishes a membrane current under an imposed voltage protocol; its contribution to natural firing requires an additional manipulation of the conductance rather than inference from its presence alone.

RNA abundance identifies a candidate molecular mechanism but does not directly measure channel current or prove that a subunit causes a behavioral transition. The expression data were therefore paired with physiological recordings and later manipulations of resurgent current.

Resurgent current increases across male development|sodium21|z4
Zemel and colleagues recorded transient and resurgent sodium currents from RA projection neurons at 20, 35, and 50 days after hatching and in adult male zebra finches. Applying the same voltage protocol across stages made the current components directly comparable.

**Space clamp** describes how well an electrode controls voltage throughout a neuron; long axons can make that control incomplete. Zemel and colleagues repeated sodium-current measurements in frontal slices that shortened the initial axonal segments of RA projection neurons.

Zemel and colleagues introduced a peptide from the intracellular C-terminal region of Navβ4 through the recording pipette into RA neurons from 20-day male zebra finches. A **peptide** is a short amino-acid chain used here to test a proposed channel interaction.

**Dynamic clamp** computes a voltage-dependent current during recording and injects that current into a real neuron. Zemel and colleagues used this method to add a resurgent-current component to juvenile RA neurons and subtract a corresponding component from adult neurons.

SK channels contribute to the afterhyperpolarization|sk12|s1
**SK channels** are small-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels. Calcium-dependent potassium efflux can produce an **afterhyperpolarization**, a period of more negative membrane voltage following a spike that regulates when the next spike occurs.
Hou and colleagues recorded RA projection neurons in adult male zebra finch slices and applied apamin, an SK-channel blocker, at 100 nM. An example response to 100 pA rose from 34 to about 53 Hz. Blocking the channels reduced the afterhyperpolarization and increased current-evoked firing, linking the pharmacological target to both the spike waveform and ongoing excitability.
The direction of the effect is consistent with removal of a potassium-mediated brake on excitation. Pharmacology establishes a contribution under the slice conditions, but does not identify every SK subunit or prove how this manipulation would change the timing of a complete song.

SK blockade makes RA firing less regular|sk12|s2
**Interspike intervals** are the times separating successive action potentials. Hou and colleagues compared spontaneous spike trains from the same RA projection neurons before and after apamin, allowing firing regularity to be assessed separately from the mean firing rate.
SK blockade increased variability in the intervals between spikes without a clear change in average spontaneous firing rate. Recordings therefore distinguished a neuron that fires more rapidly from a neuron that fires with more reproducible timing; these are different physiological properties.
Calcium-activated potassium feedback can stabilize the return toward spike threshold after each action potential. The result supports a contribution to temporal regularity in isolated RA cells, but natural song-related precision also depends on the timing of HVC and LMAN inputs and cannot be assigned entirely to SK channels.

SK blockade destabilizes evoked spike threshold|sk12|s3
A **spike threshold** is the membrane voltage at which regenerative excitation initiates an action potential. Hou and colleagues evoked firing with a 100 pA depolarizing current and compared threshold measurements before and after 100 nM apamin blocked SK channels.
Blockade increased the variation of evoked spike thresholds and shifted them toward more depolarized voltages as injected current increased. The recordings linked irregular firing with less stable spike initiation rather than assuming that every change in spike count reflected the same membrane mechanism.
The measured effect supports a role for calcium-activated potassium feedback in recovery between spikes. It does not identify the complete sequence of sodium-channel recovery or the calcium source activating SK channels, and spontaneous and current-evoked firing need not respond identically to the same intervention.

Hou and colleagues blocked ionotropic glutamate receptors while recording RA projection neurons. **AMPA receptors** support relatively fast excitatory transmission, whereas **NMDA receptors** have slower kinetics and voltage-dependent gating; the antagonists DNQX and APV suppress these receptor components.

Intrinsic potassium conductance and incoming excitation therefore interact in determining spike timing.
